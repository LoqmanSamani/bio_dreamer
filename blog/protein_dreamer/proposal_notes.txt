Model-based RL learns a model of the environment that is used as a simulator to provide additionalpseudo-observations.

[12] (4.2 ProteinGym — Large-Scale Benchmarks for Protein Fitness Prediction and Design)
---
ProteinGym serves as the primary evaluation benchmark for ProteinDreamer, providing a large-scale, standardized platform to assess protein fitness prediction and design.
1. Benchmark Scale and Diversity
The benchmark encompasses over 2.7 million mutated sequences across more than 200 protein families, providing the scale necessary for robust model evaluation. It includes:
    217 substitution DMS assays: High-throughput assays evaluating the functional impact of single or multiple amino acid replacements.
    66 indel assays (DMS): Assessing insertions and deletions, which are technically challenging due to sequence length alterations.
    Assay Diversity: The collection spans diverse taxa (humans, other eukaryotes, prokaryotes, and viruses) and a wide array of functional 
    properties, including ligand binding, aggregation, thermostability, viral replication, and drug resistance.

2. Evaluation Metrics
ProteinGym utilizes a structured analytics framework with metrics tailored to specific tasks:
    Fitness Prediction: Spearman’s rank correlation is the primary metric, as it captures the often non-linear relationship between sequence and fitness. 
    In bimodal cases, AUC and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) are used to evaluate binarized labels.
    Protein Design: Metrics focus on identifying the most functional variants:
        NDCG (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain): Up-weights models that correctly rank high-value sequences at the top.
        Top-K Recall: Measures the ability to identify the top 10% of high-fitness sequences.

3. Training Regimes and Splits
The benchmark integrates two complementary settings to evaluate model performance:
    Zero-Shot Setting: Models predict mutation effects without access to ground-truth labels for the protein of interest during training, testing robust generalization.
    Supervised Setting: Models leverage labeled subsets for training. To prevent overestimating performance due to data leakage, ProteinGym employs three cross-validation schemes:
        Random: Mutations are randomly assigned to folds.
        Contiguous: Splits sequences into five contiguous segments to test extrapolation to unseen regions.
        Modulo: Assigns positions to folds based on a modulo operator.



[13] (4.3 Multi-Scale Representation Learning for Protein Fitness Prediction)
---
Designing novel functional proteins crucially depends on accurately modeling theirfitness landscape.  
Given the limited availability of functional annotations fromwet-lab experiments, previous methods have 
primarily relied on self-supervisedmodels trained on vast, unlabeled protein sequence or structure datasets.  
Whileinitial protein representation learning studies solely focused on either sequenceor  structural  features,  
recent  hybrid  architectures  have  sought  to  merge  thesemodalities to harness their respective strengths. 
However, these sequence-structuremodels have so far achieved only incremental improvements when compared to theleading 
sequence-only approaches, highlighting unresolved challenges effectivelyleveraging these modalities together.  
Moreover, the function of certain proteinsis highly dependent on the granular aspects of their surface topology, 
which havebeen overlooked by prior models.   To address these limitations,  we introducethe  Sequence-Structure-Surface  
Fitness  (S3F)  model  —  a  novel  multimodalrepresentation learning framework that integrates protein features across 
severalscales. Our approach combines sequence representations from a protein languagemodel with Geometric Vector Perceptron 
networks encoding protein backbone anddetailed surface topology. The proposed method achieves state-of-the-art fitnessprediction 
on the ProteinGym benchmark.

1. Multi-Scale Architecture (S3F)
S3F achieves SOTA performance by stacking three distinct modalities:
    Sequence (PLM): It uses ESM-2-650M as a frozen backbone to provide high-dimensional residue embeddings. 
    These capture broad evolutionary signals but often lack granular structural awareness.
    Structure (GVP): Residue embeddings from ESM-2 initialize nodes in a Geometric Vector Perceptron (GVP) network. 
    This structure encoder performs message passing on a radius graph of alpha carbons, capturing spatial relationships within a 10A˚ neighborhood.
    Surface (dMaSIF): The model uses dMaSIF to generate a differentiable point cloud of the protein surface (6K-20K points). 
    It extracts geometric features like Gaussian curvatures and Heat Kernel Signatures, which are critical for modeling protein-solvent interactions and binding pockets.

2. Integration of MSA and PLM Features
While S3F is powerful as a "family-agnostic" model, its performance peaks when combined with family-specific information:
    S3F-MSA: By ensembling S3F predictions with EVE (an MSA-based VAE), the model improves the SOTA on ProteinGym by 8.5% in Spearman's rank correlation.
    Complementary Strengths: PLMs excel at general patterns, while MSAs capture nuanced, family-specific evolutionary constraints. 
    S3F uses structure and surface data to "correct" biases in sequence-only models, particularly for viral proteins or sequences with low MSA depth.

3. Key Insights for ProteinDreamer
    Zero-Shot Logic: Like ESM-2, S3F uses masked language modeling (MLM) for zero-shot inference. It predicts the likelihood of a 
    mutant residue at a masked position given the surrounding sequence, structure, and surface context.
    Fixed Backbone Assumption: To prevent "information leakage" (cheating by seeing the answer in the side chains), 
    S3F assumes the backbone is fixed post-mutation and omits side-chain atoms during inference.
    Epistasis: The multi-scale approach is significantly better at capturing epistatic effects (interactions between multiple mutations) 
    than sequence-only models. This is vital if your agent is designing proteins with many simultaneous changes.
    Efficiency: Despite its complexity, S3F only has 20M trainable parameters (the GVP layers), allowing it to be pre-trained in days on 
    commodity hardware—making it a practical choice for a reward head.

[15] (6.1 Tsuboyama et al. — Mega-Scale Stability Measurements)
---
The study by Tsuboyama et al. (2023) introduces cDNA display proteolysis, a massively parallel assay that generated a dataset of 776,298 
high-quality absolute folding stability (ΔG) measurements. This dataset, significantly richer than previous thermodynamic databases, 
covers all single amino acid variants (including deletions and insertions) for 331 natural and 148 de novo designed protein domains, 
along with 210,118 selected double mutants across 190 domains. The domains range from 40 to 72 amino acids in length and represent a 
diverse structural space, including nearly all suitable small monomeric PDB domains and various de novo topologies like ααα, αββα, 
and trRosetta hallucinations. The methodology proceeds in four primary steps: 
    (1) Library Synthesis: Synthetic DNA oligonucleotide pools are used to encode up to 900,000 unique protein sequences; 
    (2) cDNA Display: These libraries are transcribed and translated in a cell-free system where each protein is covalently
        linked to its own cDNA at the C-terminus; 
    (3) Protease Challenge: The protein-cDNA complexes are incubated with varying concentrations of two orthogonal proteases, 
        trypsin and chymotrypsin, which preferentially cleave unfolded proteins ; and 
    (4) Quantification: Intact (resistant) proteins are pulled down via an N-terminal tag, and their 
        relative abundance is measured using next-generation sequencing (NGS). 
To ensure low noise and high accuracy, the researchers employed a Bayesian kinetic model to infer ΔG from the protease concentration at which 
half-maximal cleavage occurs (K50​), specifically accounting for sequence-specific protease susceptibility in the unfolded state. 
This rigorous approach resulted in high reproducibility (R=0.94 between independent protease experiments) and strong agreement 
with traditional gold-standard purified protein measurements.

7. Active Inference & Free Energy Principle — Theoretical Backbone


[17] (7.2 Reinforcement Learning Through Active Inference)
---
The central tenet of reinforcement learning (RL) is that agents seek to maximizethe sum of cumulative rewards.  In contrast, active inference, 
an emerging frame-work within cognitive and computational neuroscience, proposes that agents actto maximize the evidence for a biased generative model.

Tschantz et al. (2020) provide a technical bridge between active inference and deep reinforcement learning (RL) by recasting reward maximization 
as the minimization of the divergence between desired and expected future outcomes. The authors introduce the Free Energy of the Expected Future (FEEF), 
a novel objective function that serves as a tractable bound on established probabilistic RL objectives while naturally unifying exploration and exploitation. 
Architecturally, the model is implemented in a model-based RL setting where agents maintain a generative model—parameterized by neural networks and deep 
ensembles—to evaluate beliefs about future states, observations, and model parameters. Policy selection is treated as an approximate inference process, 
optimized using the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) to identify action sequences that minimize the FEEF. The core objective is formulated as:

G(pi) = sum_{t} E_{q(s_t, theta | pi)} [ log q(theta | s_t, pi) - log p(s_t, theta) ]

This FEEF objective decomposes into two critical components that justify its use over standard RL:

G(pi) approx sum_{t} ( - E_{q(s_t | pi)} [ log p(s_t) ] - E_{q(theta)} [ I(s_t; theta) ] )

The first term represents extrinsic value (reward seeking), while the second term represents expected information gain (uncertainty reduction), 
which promotes directed exploration in both state and parameter space without the need for ad-hoc exploratory terms. This framework is particularly 
superior for protein design because it inherently addresses the challenge of navigating expensive, partially observed fitness landscapes: 
by minimizing the FEEF, the agent automatically prioritizes protein sequences that resolve model uncertainty (exploration) while simultaneously driving 
toward high-fitness regions (exploitation), ensuring efficient optimization when every "query" to the landscape is costly.


[18] (7.3 Active Inference: Demystified and Compared)
---
Sajid et al. (2020) provide a formal distinction between active inference and reinforcement learning (RL) by shifting the fundamental driver 
of behavior from external reward signals to internal generative models. While standard RL is defined by the reward hypothesis—where agents 
maximize a scalar signal—active inference agents minimize variational free energy (F) and expected free energy (G), treating rewards as "prior preferences" 
over sensory observations. This framework is superior for protein design because it eliminates the need for engineered exploration heuristics; instead, 
the minimization of expected free energy naturally balances extrinsic value (seeking high-fitness sequences) with intrinsic value (epistemic exploration 
to reduce model uncertainty). In this paradigm, the generative model and its priors drive behavior rather than an external reward function, allowing the 
agent to proactively query the most informative regions of a partially observed and expensive fitness landscape. Unlike Bayesian RL, which only seeks information 
to increase future rewards, active inference justifies exploration as a first-principle requirement for maintaining an accurate world model, ensuring the ProteinDreamer 
architecture remains robust even when experimental data is sparse.

Core Equations for Implementation
To implement these distinctions, the following free energy functionals are used:
1. Variational Free Energy (F) (for perception and state estimation):
F = D_KL[Q(s|pi) || P(s|pi)] - E_{Q(s|pi)}[log P(o|s)]
(Decomposes into complexity minus accuracy)

2. Expected Free Energy (G) (for policy selection and planning):
Plaintext
G(pi) approx sum_tau ( E_{Q(o_tau, s_tau | pi)} [log Q(s_tau | pi) - log P(o_tau, s_tau)] )
(Minimizing G ensures the agent seeks outcomes that are both informative and preferred)

3. Prior Preferences as Utility:
Plaintext
P(o) = sigma(U(o))
(Utility is framed as a log-probability distribution over outcomes, making it a feature of the agent rather than the environment)




8. Bayesian Optimisation & Active Learning for Protein Engineering


[19] (8.1 Adaptive Machine Learning for Protein Engineering)
---
Hie and Yang (2022) review the "adaptive machine learning" framework for protein engineering, which formalizes a closed-loop iterative design 
process consisting of four key components: a target property, a sequence-to-function surrogate model (e.g., Gaussian processes or neural networks), 
a generative proposer to navigate the combinatorial sequence space, and an acquisition function to prioritize variants for experimental testing. 
While the community widely employs greedy acquisition for its simplicity, the authors highlight that the field is increasingly adopting Bayesian 
optimization to better balance the tradeoff between exploitation (seeking known high-fitness regions) and exploration (querying high-uncertainty regions to improve the model). 
This loop—where a surrogate predicts function, an acquisition function like Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) or Expected Improvement (EI) selects variants, 
and the model is retrained on new wet-lab data—parallels the ProteinDreamer architecture. By explicitly discussing the potential to reformulate this 
objective as a reinforcement learning problem, the authors suggest a path toward more sophisticated decision-making machinery that goes beyond traditional 
iterative design to proactively manage the uncertainty of expensive fitness landscapes.

[24] (1.3 Causal-JEPA — Learning World Models through Object-Level Latent Interventions)
---
Nam et al. (2026) introduce Causal-JEPA (C-JEPA), a reconstruction-free world model that extends the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) 
by incorporating causal reasoning through object-level latent interventions. Unlike traditional world models like DreamerV3 that rely on computationally 
expensive structure reconstruction (RSSM), C-JEPA operates entirely in latent space by using object-level masking as a proxy for causal interventions, 
forcing the model to infer an entity's trajectory solely from its interactions with other objects. In the ProteinDreamer framework, mutations are treated 
as causal interventions on the protein state; the JEPA encoder maps a sequence and its predicted structure to a latent representation (zt​), while the 
predictor forecasts the next state (z^t+1​) conditioned on a specific mutation. The model is trained using an energy-based loss function that minimizes 
the distance between the predicted embedding and the target embedding of the mutated state, formulated as:

L = || Pred(z_context, mutation) - Enc(z_target) ||^2

This objective, often augmented with variance and covariance regularization (such as VICReg) to prevent informational collapse, 
ensures that the world model learns a stable, intervention-aware fitness landscape. This framework is highly efficient—achieving 
comparable performance to patch-based models with only 1% of the total latent input features—and aligns naturally with Active Inference,
as both paradigms prioritize minimizing the discrepancy (variational free energy) between internal predictions and realized latent states 
rather than high-fidelity observation-space reconstruction.



[25] (1.4 LeWorldModel — Stable End-to-End Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture from Pixels)
---
Maes et al. (2026) introduce LeWorldModel (LeWM), a robust Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) that provides the foundational 
training methodology for ProteinDreamer by overcoming the inherent stability and collapse challenges of latent-space world models. 
Unlike reconstruction-based models like DreamerV3 that require computationally expensive decoders to rebuild observations (e.g., full protein structures), 
LeWM operates entirely in a compact latent space, enabling planning speeds up to 48x faster while matching or exceeding the performance of traditional architectures. 
The methodology follows a streamlined three-step recipe:

    (1) Joint-Embedding, where an encoder maps the protein state (sequence and structure) into a latent space; 
    (2) Latent Prediction, where a predictor forecasts the next embedding conditioned on a mutation (action) without any observation-space reconstruction; and 
    (3) Isotropic Regularization, which ensures a well-behaved, non-collapsed manifold. 

This stability is achieved through a simplified training loss consisting of only a next-embedding prediction error and a Gaussian regularizer (SIGReg) to prevent representation collapse:

L = || Pred(z_t, action) - z_{t+1} ||^2 + lambda * SIGReg(z)

By demonstrating that this end-to-end objective naturally induces "temporal latent straightening"—where latent trajectories become more linear and predictable—LeWM 
justifies ProteinDreamer’s shift from the complex RSSM machinery of DreamerV3 to a more efficient, intervention-aware JEPA framework. 
This transition is critical for protein design, as it allows the world model to maintain a stable and queryable fitness landscape without 
the overhead of generating high-fidelity structural coordinates at every optimization step.



[26] (1.5 PointWorld — Scaling 3D World Models for In-The-Wild Robotic Manipulation)
---
Huang et al. (2026) introduce PointWorld, a large-scale 3D world model that predicts physical dynamics by operating directly in observation space using 3D point clouds and point flows. 
The methodology follows a four-step pipeline: 
    (1) State-Action Unification: where both the initial scene and the proposed robot actions are represented as 3D point flows in a shared spatial coordinate system; 
    (2) Geometric Encoding: utilizing a scalable architecture (such as a 3D-aware Transformer) to process the point-cloud inputs; 
    (3) Dynamics Forecasting: where the model predicts the full-scene 3D point flows representing the environment's transformation; and 
    (4) Pre-training: involving large-scale training across diverse real and simulated manipulation datasets to enable zero-shot generalization. 
In the ProteinDreamer proposal, PointWorld represents a distinct paradigm from the JEPA-based approach; whereas PointWorld performs explicit 3D reasoning in coordinate 
space—analogous to running an intensive structure predictor for every mutation—ProteinDreamer prioritizes Causal-JEPA’s latent-space prediction for its superior computational efficiency. 
While PointWorld’s high-fidelity structural modeling is conceptually appealing for structure-aware design, the latent-space route remains more practical given the prohibitive 
cost of 3D evaluation at each optimization step, though PointWorld serves as a vital blueprint for future high-fidelity ProteinDreamer 
variants that may prioritize structural accuracy over inference speed.
